This is not an apple is a clothing collection about consumer culture that emerged from encountering Masao Saito’s airbrushed commercial food illustrations (Figure 1) and the idea of redeveloping them using generative AI. It is a distillation of the designers’ experiences with, reflections on, and consumption of Japanese Bubble Era consumer aesthetics (~1978 to 1991).1 The primary goal was to produce something new in the form of a common commercial product – tailored shirts – while avoiding common design pitfalls such as preservation of the past, strict cultural authenticity, or retrofuturist pastiche.

Consumer aesthetics in Japan during the Bubble Era were influenced by the economic prosperity and asset inflation during the 1980s (Watkins, n.d.). As clients and consumers were economically advantaged, designers were able to realize their most experimental and radical ideas, leading to Japan becoming globally influential in design. The dominant aesthetic language of the period was postmodern, not only because of chronology but also because the leading Japanese avant-garde designers shared a common denial of modernism’s formal unity, favouring emergent coherence amongst other postmodern tendencies (Bartal, 2015). For example, Rei Kawakubo’s Comme des Garçons was rhizomatic: garments were full of holes, stores were confrontational, furniture was transitory, and publications like SIX resembled enigmatic art catalogues rather than advertising (Sudjic, 1990). Yet a coherent sensibility emerged when taken together.

During the ideation stage of This is not an apple, an encounter with Michele De Lucchi’s Continental side table for Memphis Milano on a recent cover of Casa Brutus prompted a dominant thought: How is it that Continental is both immediately recognizable as a cow and a distinctly postmodern object?

Answering this was crucial to the project’s aim of re-establishing its source material within a contemporary wardrobe. Continental is not representative of Bubble Era consumer aesthetics, but it does share a common postmodern design language. Understanding how postmodern consumer objects were recognized would provide a clear departure point for reinterpreting Bubble Era consumer aesthetics.

What Continental revealed was complex reduction, a recurring tendency within postmodernism that differentiated it from modernism. Modernism tended to reduce objects by subordinating their constituent elements to a formalized, functional order. Complex reduction begins from this modernist paradigm, further deconstructing, refining, and recombining those elements toward a different endpoint. The subject becomes simpler than its original, while the resulting postmodern object becomes compositionally and semantically denser.

Complex reduction served as an indispensable creative method for This is not an apple, as it made it possible to develop design solutions while remaining related to the source material.

The Process of Complex Reduction

Complex reduction (Figure 2) names the process through which deconstruction, refinement, and semiotic recombination produce the postmodern object. As objects retain traces of the processes through which they are produced, so too does the resulting postmodern object. Its surfaces and relations preserve evidence of the operations through which it was developed.

Deconstruction can be understood as taking something apart, stripping it to the constituent elements and relations through which it becomes recognizable (Derrida, 1967/2016). This does not only mean physically dismantling the object, something more akin to disassembly or decomposition. Rather, it relies on an examination of the tensions and dependencies within its existing structure (Johnson & Wigley, 1988).

Refinement is a prolonged, staged process through which selected qualities are isolated, clarified, and concentrated toward an idealized condition. A new object is then progressively smoothed out and developed according to the logic of its existing, that is to say modern, form in order to produce something novel.

Semiotic recombination is the process by which signifiers are separated from their original contexts and recombined within a new object, allowing multiple meanings and associations to operate simultaneously.

Representational images, through deconstruction and refinement, may become simpler than the original naturalistic form. A cow, for example, can be reduced and refined in form and detail to only its most essential and limited set of recognizable characteristics: bodily mass, anatomical relations, black-and-white hide, and so on. This constitutes the first movement from premodern naturalistic depiction toward modernist abstraction. The object may then become more complex through the semiotic recombination of the revealed relations. These operations create an object more complex than a strict modernist interpretation would permit, while still employing the operational logic of modernism (Baudrillard, 1968/1996; Jencks, 1991; Venturi, 1966/2019).

Complex reduction produces low representational granularity but high compositional and semantic density and is a recurring, although not universal, aspect of postmodern design.

The Modern Gesamtkunstwerk and Its Postmodern Reversal

Postmodernism can be understood as a constitutive reversal of modernism (Venturi, 1966/2019). However, postmodernism does not restore what modernism removed. This reversal is more like a Möbius strip; it changes direction while acting upon the structural logic of modernism: abstraction, reduction of form, and industrial production (Gropius, 1919/1974; Rams, 1995) to advance modernism toward an intensified form.

The basic movement describing this reversal is as follows:

Premodern: Decorative ornamentation based in naturalistic depiction, or basic abstraction of forms
Modernism: Reduction and subordination of ornament and constituent elements to a unified formal order
Postmodernism: Reintroduction of complexity via the logic of modernism.

Postmodernism increased complexity while retaining the language of modernism, rather than retrieving premodern styles. Even when retrieval exists, it is postmodern in nature, primarily appearing as quotation (pastiche) or idiomatic expression (Jencks, 1991; Venturi, 1966/2019; Rossi, 1981). Quotation reintroduces historical forms as self-conscious referents, while idiomatic expression retrieves the relations of classicism without necessarily reproducing their original appearance. These postmodern operations depended upon inherited modernist formalism.

The basis of this reversal rests on modernism’s subordination of elements to the unified logic of the whole, regardless of the whole’s scale. In modernism, objects were unified according to the logic of the object itself (form follows function), then unified within their environments, while environments were unified within the overarching logic of the whole project. Modernism’s recursive unification is an application of the proto-modern concept of the Gesamtkunstwerk. Emerging from Romanticism and later formalized in its best-known form by Wagner, the Gesamtkunstwerk was an attempt to unite multiple artistic forms and media within a singular, all-encompassing work (Finger & Follett, 2011; Imhoof et al., 2016). Modernism adapted this idea toward its own ends. Unity was developed through the organization of individual elements or the governance of a complete project or object. This recursive unification is evident in objects and projects such as Eileen Gray’s E.1027 adjustable side table and Arne Jacobsen’s SAS Royal Hotel.

Eileen Gray’s side table, in particular, represents a singular element within the complete project logic of E.1027 (Figure 6) while also standing alone as its own object. The table coheres with its intended function and with the larger Gray-designed E.1027 site (O’Neil Ford Monograph Series, 2018).

Jacobsen’s SAS Royal Hotel (Figure 7) extended this same principle across the scale of a complete architectural and commercial environment encompassing all the constituent elements of a hotel (Sheridan, 2011).

Postmodernism began from this recursively unified foundation but loosened and multiplied the relationships among its elements, making more surfaces independently available for recombination of the object or environment (O’Neil Ford Monograph Series, 2018; Sheridan, 2011).

Cow Architecture

Postmodern design increased the resolution of modernism’s inherited framework across several dimensions: formally through more independently legible components; materially, through an expanded material palette; productively, through new fabrication and production techniques; and semantically, through juxtaposition and reference (Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2004; Venturi, 1966/2019; Victoria and Albert Museum, 2023).

In postmodern design, semiotic recombination allows these components to separate and collide more freely, or to carry several meanings at once, in a way that was both a reaction against and extension of modernism. The strict system of modernism is subverted and exploited, then oriented toward exhausting its possibilities.

The process of semiotic recombination can be demonstrated through a ten-image comparative series of historically dispersed pictorial or objectual depictions of cows, revealing this shared tendency.

In the above example set, the cow is introduced as a representative premodern pastoral subject in Aelbert Cuyp’s Cows and Herdsman by a River, which situates the cow within a familiar atmosphere. The cow is read as a complete animal in a complete environment, reflecting and signifying a real scene through a pictorial description of realistic naturalism (Wheelock, 2001). In the second image, Charles Turner’s The Yorkshire Rose, the landscape recedes, and the viewer’s gaze is fixed on a partially abstracted cow. The body’s anatomical form is exaggerated into a large rectangular form, while the rest of the image remains firmly rooted in naturalistic depiction (Christie's, n.d.). In the third, Arthur Dove’s Cow advances this series by depicting a cow as a mass of heavily abstracted formal qualities, overlapping curves, and tonal masses in an ambiguous spatial relationship. The cow is still recognizable but is no longer depicted naturalistically (Haskell, 1974; Metropolitan Museum of Art, n.d.).

The fourth and fifth images come from Picasso’s series Le Taureau, a set of lithographs depicting a bull in increasingly deconstructed stages (Museum of Modern Art, 2010). The fourth image is an intermediate state in which the basic form of the bull has been reorganized into a geometric system of a bovine’s internal divisions and structural relationships. Despite this highly abstracted state, the basic form, anatomy, and legibility remain. The fifth image contains the final reduced state of the bull. A body consisting of fewer than a dozen lines is nevertheless still perfectly comprehensible as a bull (Museum of Modern Art, 2010; Penrose, 1958).

In Composition VIII (The Cow) by Theo van Doesburg, the cow has been fully subordinated to van Doesburg’s modernist system. The cow is recognizable as a spatially arranged set of rectangles relying on proportion, rhythm, and colour (Museum of Modern Art, 1982; Overy, 1991).

At this point in the sequence, postmodern reversal begins to appear. Starting from the reduction of the modernist middle, the individual elements of the cow become available for recombination.  Roy Lichtenstein’s Cow Going Abstract references Picasso’s Le Taureau while employing Lichtenstein’s trademark Pop Art and comic-book vernacular to reimagine the cow in successive stages of deconstruction and increasing abstraction (Alloway, 1983; Roy Lichtenstein Foundation, n.d.). Andy Warhol’s Cow references premodern themes such as pastoralism and agrarian imagery but transposes them into the form of a mass-produced consumer good (Museum of Modern Art, n.d.; Warhol & Hackett, 1980).

François-Xavier Lalanne’s Vache Paysage, or “cow landscape”, produces a cow as a design object and sculpture with a flat top resembling a console or table. An empty rectangular frame allows the viewer to see through the cow, framing the landscape in a humorous reversal of typical pastoral imagery, in which cows are a complement to the landscape (Sotheby’s, 2023).

Cows are not recognized through an inventory of anatomical properties. The cow’s cow-ness lies in the world of formal and cultural relations gathered by those properties and collected in the accumulated history of cattle imagery (Heidegger, 1950/1971, 1967). This is how an anatomically incorrect child’s drawing may still be recognized as a cow: it gathers enough of the relations constitutive of cow-ness to be legible.

What is a cow architecturally? A cow already has several qualities associated with domestic furniture, specifically tables and chairs. Cows have four legs and a long horizontal body; you can sit on a cow or place objects on its back with relative ease. Were it not for the fact that it is a living, breathing animal, it could make a serviceable chair or table. Therefore, the relationship between cow and table is not completely arbitrary. It is based on an existing formal and functional analogy. The immediate reaction upon seeing the final image in the sequence, Michele De Lucchi’s Continental, is something like, “Oh, it’s a cow,” but how does this happen? And how did we arrive at this recognition through a specifically postmodern process (Radice, 1984)?

Continental, designed for Memphis Milano in 1984, is the final refinement of the cow as an ideal postmodern object in the comparative series. The table is made of wood covered in laminate and is constructed from several intersecting geometric prisms. In Continental, the cow has been reduced to one of its simplest and most recognizable signifiers: its black-and-white hide pattern, while the cow’s four legs are compressed into two supports, modifying both material and form. The table does not look literally like a cow, but its pattern, structure, and supports preserve enough information for the association to remain legible (Radice, 1984).

The analytic series presented above is an acute example of complex reduction. The cow becomes representationally simpler while the composition becomes more complex in a logically congruent and constrained way. The set of relationships making up the cow can then be further modified and recombined while retaining its underlying referent and associations (Baudrillard, 1968/1996; Jencks, 1991).

The movement from modernism to postmodernism as demonstrated in the ten-image analytical series can be summarized as the shift from a rectangle to a polygon. If the modernist object is geometric, reduced to its basic elements, the postmodern parallel increases the number of visible vertices and surfaces. Postmodern design increases the number of independently legible elements and relations within the object (Jencks, 1991).

The cow becomes less of a cow as a biological image, but more densely cow-like as a sign. The column does not return to a classical form, except through quotation; it becomes a complex polygon either through surface, meaning, or both. In postmodern design, the act of reduction becomes complex.

Complex Reduction as Design Method

In This is not an apple, complex reduction was used primarily in the collection's graphic development. The formal and cultural elements of Bubble Era consumer goods were deconstructed, refined, and recombined in several ways. The original scans of Masao Saito's work were run through generative AI tools to produce images of items he had never illustrated, including specific crab compositions, different kinds of produce, and denser floral blooms.

Despite the obvious differences from Saito’s original work, the first generations were insufficiently developed to meet the collection's end goal. They did not feel truly original or additive. In fact, the most compelling images from the initial generations were those that inadvertently revealed the process of generative AI in overt ways: imprecise prompts that produced crabs with too many limbs, apples with too many leaves, food with bizarre, surreal, or unnatural anatomy, and so on. It became clear that what could be produced that was genuinely new, or at least significantly more compelling than simple reproduction, came from ‘breaking’ the AI's attempts to produce the desired subject realistically.

The second and third rounds of image generation were designed to exploit and accelerate these generative AI ‘failings’ and to ‘lean into’ the medium.

One of the threads that broke the realism came from considering the rectangle-becoming-a-polygon summary and coming across the cover of George Benson's The Shape of Things to Come, which features a graphic arrangement of basic three-dimensional shapes (Benson, 1968). In addition to pushing the range and complexity of the objects generated from simple food products, sets of prompts shifted toward creating illustrations that forced objects to conform to strict polygonal forms such as cubes, cones, prisms, and truncated pyramids. New textures were introduced to increase the complexity of relations and induce confusion in the viewer.

Confusion or disorientation is something that AI is uniquely adept at. AI can create images that appear familiar yet lack a clear point of reference, making them nearly impossible to focus on or fully resolve. Attempting to capture this ‘anti-focal point’ or ‘impossible’ effect became incredibly desirable. However, the collection's frame of reference was mass consumer goods, so total abstraction of the kind described above was introduced only to a limited extent through texture and hybridity. Images were created in which foods transformed into other fruit species in ways that inspired both disgust and desire: a banana covered in jam, or a piece of fish cut into a perfect pyramid like some kind of deranged terrine. These images retained the commercial legibility of food illustration while introducing impossible juxtapositions which induced a level of disorientation or confusion without pushing the images into total illegibility.

When it came to packaging, care tags, hangtags, and similar components, quoting logomarks and consumer packaging was a natural starting point. Generative AI was then applied to approximately 200 images drawn from auction listings of Bubble Era packaging across a wide range of consumer goods, including electronics, batteries, candy, confectionery, chips, ramen, musical instruments, and canned drinks. From these references, thousands of variations were generated in ‘sheets’. The most promising variations were then sorted into idiosyncratic sets of relations and associations to capture the full range of consumer packaging that was studied. The development of the collection's own packaging proceeded from there, using the generated labels and tags as new compositional references.

Packaging was selected because it felt representative of the Bubble Era's overarching aesthetic tendencies, rather than consumer packaging that had simply been released during that period.

For example, Seiko's wordmark or corporate identity was not considered representative, but many of Seiko's print advertisements from the period were studied, and their layouts, surfaces, typography, colour, and product presentation were used to inform subsequent generations.

Understood through the terminology established above, the design process followed the three operations of complex reduction. Once consumer product references were selected, they were deconstructed into independently legible elements and relations, from which sets of prompts were developed to explore those elements in isolation and in combination. These elements were then refined and recombined through extensive image generation, selection, sorting, and editing (the first two rounds alone produced nearly 50 GB of images). As in the movement from naturalistic depictions of the cow to Continental, the resulting images became less faithful to any single original referent while becoming semantically denser.

This process was largely executed and iterated using large language models to compose sets of prompts derived from designer-identified traits and elements, rapidly testing and exploring ideas and their recombination through generative AI tools. The underlying logic of This is not an apple, including the identification of the source material's constituent elements, did not come from any kind of AI, but from the designers' own observations and synthesis of ideas. Generative AI allowed for rapid iteration, but the designers' recognition of AI’s ‘failings,’ and of the ways those failings could be exploited to produce unexpected results, provided the way those graphics were ultimately developed.

Identifying complex reduction provided a method that allowed the collection to move well beyond its source material while remaining connected to it. Essentially, the source material contained the means of its own transformation.
